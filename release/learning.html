Try a two-minute question +

Your starting point

For a fixed two-outcome quantum measurement, must the probabilities of its possible outcomes add up to one?

Compare your reasoning

Yes. The probabilities of all possible outcomes of the specified measurement must sum to one. Changing the state can change those probabilities; the measurement setup defines the outcomes being considered.

Try a two-minute question +

Your starting point

A steady, incompressible flow passes through a pipe with no leaks. If its cross-sectional area halves, what happens to the mean flow speed?

Compare your reasoning

It doubles. Under these assumptions the volume flow rate is constant, so area multiplied by mean speed stays the same. The conclusion depends on the stated steady-flow, incompressibility, and no-leak assumptions.

Try a two-minute question +

Your starting point

A charged particle moves exactly parallel to a uniform magnetic field. With no electric field present, is the magnetic force zero?

Compare your reasoning

Yes. The magnetic force is proportional to the cross product of velocity and magnetic field. Parallel vectors give a zero cross product. This single-particle example is a starting point, not a complete plasma model.

Try a two-minute question +

Your starting point

For a plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum, how are the electric field, magnetic field, and propagation direction arranged?

Compare your reasoning

The electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to one another, and both are perpendicular to the direction of propagation. This description applies to the plane-wave setup specified in the question.

Work with events, reference frames, and light cones to ask what can influence what—and how a diagram encodes it.

  • Events

  1. 01Locate an event

Try a two-minute question +

Your starting point

In a spacetime diagram, what does the light cone around an event help you distinguish?

Compare your reasoning

It distinguishes events that can be causally connected to the chosen event from events outside that causal reach. A light cone describes possible influence; it is not a picture of an object moving faster than light.

Try a two-minute question +

Your starting point

If P implies Q, and Q is true, must P also be true?

Compare your reasoning

No. The converse needs its own justification. For example, every multiple of 4 is even, but 6 is even without being a multiple of 4. One counterexample is enough to disprove a claim that is supposed to hold in every case.
